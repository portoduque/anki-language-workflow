# Justin Sung — “How to use Anki like a PRO (by a Learning Expert)” — Selective Adaptation Note

Source analyzed:

- YouTube: https://youtu.be/eauQac_23R0
- Channel: Justin Sung
- Published: 2023-10-03
- Duration: 20:31

The complete spoken transcript was reviewed from start to finish, including:

- benefits of retrieval, spacing, and micro-learning;
- limitations of one-to-one flashcard retrieval;
- flashcard overload;
- memorizing the card wording instead of the underlying knowledge;
- the proposed targeted-review / consolidation / preview workflow;
- flagging cards after repeated successes/failures;
- deeper review of repeatedly failed cards;
- merging mastered cards into higher-order questions;
- previewing upcoming material.

## What already matches this project

### Flashcards are a tool, not the whole learning system

The video argues that flashcards are useful for retrieval and spacing but should not replace deeper learning/application.

This project already states that Anki should make learned material retrievable and **does not replace reading, listening, speaking, or writing practice**.

For language learning, this distinction is especially important:
- Reading cards do not replace reading;
- Listening cards do not replace real listening;
- Production cards do not replace speaking/writing;
- Pronunciation cards do not replace actual speech practice.

No architecture change is required.

### Volume can overwhelm the learner

The video warns that putting everything into flashcards creates a workload trap.

This is already a core project rule:
- candidate ≠ card;
- zero cards is valid;
- every extra card has future review cost;
- creation time also counts;
- optimize memory efficiency, not volume.

### Repeated failure should trigger diagnosis

The video recommends taking repeatedly failed cards out of blind repetition and examining them more deeply.

The project already treats leeches/repeated failures as diagnostics:
- ambiguous prompt?
- overloaded target?
- missing context?
- wrong media/answer?
- low-value item?

The new adaptation below adds one language-specific repair option: improve meaningful **relationships/contrasts**, not just add more repetitions.

## Useful ideas adopted

### 1. Atomic retrieval can still be relational

The video's strongest conceptual point is that isolated fact recall is not the only useful retrieval target. Sometimes the learner needs the **relationship** between pieces of knowledge.

Literal “mega cards” are not adopted, but the principle transfers well to languages.

A relationship can itself be one atomic target, for example:
- choosing between two confusable prepositions;
- distinguishing two near-synonyms;
- choosing tense/aspect based on context;
- discriminating two grammatical constructions;
- recognizing why one collocation fits and another does not.

Adaptation:
- allow relational/comparison cards when the relationship is the one useful retrieval target;
- keep the prompt constrained;
- do not ask several independent translations/conjugations/explanations at once.

This preserves the project’s atomicity rule while allowing higher-value discrimination.

### 2. Avoid memorizing the card rather than the language

The video describes learners recognizing a repeated flashcard surface pattern and producing the answer without retrieving the underlying knowledge flexibly.

This maps directly to transfer in language learning.

Adaptation:
- avoid stereotyped preambles or accidental unique cues that reveal the answer;
- use natural context that forces the intended distinction;
- when more than one card is independently justified, vary natural contexts or retrieval directions rather than cloning the same wording;
- do not create variants solely to increase volume.

Relevant evidence:

- Butler et al. found that retrieval practice with **different examples** produced better transfer to new examples than repeatedly retrieving the same example:
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29265856/
- Pagán & Nation found benefits from contextual diversity when adults learned new words from reading:
  https://doi.org/10.1111/cogs.12705
- Lauro, Schwartz & Francis found better recall for novel words encountered in varied semantic contexts:
  https://doi.org/10.1016/j.jml.2020.104123
- A 2026 foreign-language vocabulary study found benefits from variable retrieval practice, especially for later difficult-direction retrieval:
  https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/42792504/

The project therefore adopts **contextual transfer**, not random variation for its own sake.

### 3. Graduate redundant scaffolds instead of accumulating forever

The video proposes merging mastered basic cards into harder relational cards.

The literal merge strategy is too broad for this project, but the underlying workload idea is useful.

Adaptation:
- when maintaining an existing collection and reliable mastery evidence exists;
- if a richer contextual card fully covers an easier scaffold;
- and the easier card no longer fills a distinct skill gap;
- the workflow may recommend retiring/suspending the redundant scaffold.

Safeguards:
- do not infer mastery from card age alone;
- do not delete user cards without permission;
- do not retire a Reading scaffold merely because Production exists unless the Reading skill is truly covered/no longer needed.

This generalizes the existing spelling/sound fade-out principle.

### 4. Repeated failure can indicate missing relationships

For a valuable item that continues to fail, the repair is not necessarily “repeat it more.”

Language-specific repair options include:
- a clearer contextual cue;
- a contrast with the confusable alternative;
- a useful collocation;
- a word-family/morphology relationship;
- a concise semantic distinction;
- a better native example.

Only add such information when it actually resolves the failure. Do not turn every back into a lesson.

## Ideas intentionally NOT adopted

### “Mega flashcards” containing several independent facts

The video suggests merging several mastered cards into a higher-order question.

For academic conceptual subjects this can sometimes be useful, but directly copying it would conflict with this project's fast-review and one-primary-target rules.

Language adaptation:
- test one relationship/decision at a time;
- do not create multi-answer mega cards.

### Fixed “three correct / three incorrect” trigger

The video recommends flagging cards after three consecutive successes or failures.

Not adopted:
- Anki/FSRS already records review performance;
- three is an arbitrary threshold;
- mastery/failure evidence should come from actual review history and card behavior rather than a universal count.

### “100–150 flashcards per week”

This is a personal workflow estimate, not a universal language-learning quota.

Not adopted. Sustainable workload remains learner-specific.

### Spend 5–10 minutes on every repeatedly failed card

The deeper-diagnosis principle is useful, but a fixed time prescription is not.

Some cards need a 20-second prompt rewrite; others need genuine concept/context work; low-value cards should simply be removed.

### Preview is universally more important than review

The video argues strongly for previewing upcoming academic material.

This may be useful in course-based study, but it is not promoted to a language-card generation rule.

For this project:
- meaningful exposure before card creation is preferred;
- beginner bootstrap sources remain allowed;
- reading/listening/speaking/writing practice remains outside Anki;
- current due reviews should not be ignored because a creator prefers previewing.

### Flashcards are inherently only “lower-order”

The video uses a strong lower-order/higher-order framing.

The project does not adopt that as an absolute claim.

Evidence shows:
- retrieval practice can improve comprehension/inference;
- varied retrieval/examples can support transfer;
- retrieval and elaborative encoding each have strengths depending on task/feedback/context.

Relevant evidence:
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/21252317/
- https://doi.org/10.1007/s10648-025-10076-6
- https://pubmed.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/29265856/

The practical project rule is narrower:
- simple flashcards are excellent for clear retrieval targets;
- relational/discriminative retrieval is allowed when that is the language knowledge;
- Anki still does not replace real language use.

### Mandatory weekly long-session workflow

The targeted review → consolidation → preview routine is a personal study-system architecture.

This skill creates and maintains language-learning cards; it does not impose a weekly lifestyle/study schedule.

### Micro-learning as a card-generation rule

Short pockets of review time can be useful, but review scheduling habits are outside the card-generation contract. The project supports fast cards so micro-review is possible without prescribing where or when the user must study.

## Net changes justified by this source

1. Clarify that **one relationship/contrast can be an atomic retrieval target**.
2. Add a rule against **cue overfitting / memorizing the card wording instead of the language**.
3. Prefer **contextual retrieval variability** when multiple independently useful cards/contexts are justified.
4. Generalize scaffold fade-out into **evidence-based retirement of redundant mastered scaffolds** during collection maintenance.
5. Add relationship/contrast/collocation repair as an option for valuable repeatedly failed cards.

No schema, builder, media provider, deck architecture, installer, or AnkiConnect implementation change is justified by this source.
